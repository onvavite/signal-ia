# Saturday, November 14, 2026 — Agents that prove theorems, glasses that film everyone — and a job market automating on both sides

*Signal IA — 8:00 PM edition. AI news, without the noise.*

One question runs through all five stories tonight: who checks, and for whom? Google says its agents proved theorems — an independent checker counted the lines and found something different from the press release. The EFF explains that smart glasses shift the burden of privacy onto bystanders. Project Zero is shortening its disclosure window because AI speeds up attackers too. And the job market is automating on both sides: agents apply on your behalf while scammers use AI to fabricate fake job listings.

## 1. Google Antigravity /teamwork: agents prove theorems — provided you check the numbers

### What is it?

Google Research published on November 13 the results of [/teamwork](https://research.google/blog/advancing-science-engineering-with-antigravitys-teamwork-feature/), the multi-agent system inside its Antigravity development environment. The idea: teams of specialist agents, powered by the Gemini 4 models (Argon, Carbon, Hydrogen), work in parallel on open problems, while "falsifiers" — the Lean 4 proof checker, unmodified test suites, simulators, hardware counters — decide which of their proposals hold up. Each agent receives only the files and tools it needs for its step: context is partitioned on purpose, so critics review results without being influenced by the producer's reasoning.

The announced scorecard: a proof of the Courtade-Kumar conjecture (2013), a formalization of the Weil conjectures, a first formalization of the Birkhoff conjecture (1927), a port of the Zircon microkernel to safe Rust, a 9x9 Go engine trained from scratch, and production infrastructure gains at Google.

### What actually changed

The most solid result is also the oldest. The proof of Courtade-Kumar — a 2013 information-theory question: can you compress a noise-damaged bit string down to a single bit without losing more information than the laziest possible method? — appeared as a preprint on September 21, 2026 ([arXiv:2609.24931](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.24931)), from researchers at Google, CUHK, USC and Carnegie Mellon. What the November post adds is the formalization: the whole proof translated into Lean 4, a language that lets a computer kernel verify every logical step. Google puts it at 50 million lines of code.

And that is where independent verification gets interesting. The site [sysnote](https://sysnote.net/news/google-s-teamwork-math-claims-checked-99-5-of-the-50-million-lean-lines-are-cert) cloned the public repository ([dpwoodru/general-courtade-kumar-lean](https://github.com/dpwoodru/general-courtade-kumar-lean)) and counted: 50,373,106 lines — the number checks out. But only 1,359 files, or 238,690 lines, are proof a human could read. The other 44,141 files (4.88 GB) are generated certificates — numerical bounds over pieces of the parameter space that the Lean kernel checks mechanically. Under half a percent of the total is proof; the rest is interval arithmetic. The audit trail itself is solid: a 63-line statement to trust, a clean rebuild with a byte-identical final file, and acceptance of all 22,107,215 declarations by con-leche, the Lean Foundation's external checker. All of it between September 23 and October 15.

The second headline result cannot be checked at all. Google says /teamwork-LEAP "fully autonomously produced an end-to-end, machine-checked formalization of the Weil conjectures" — the landmark results on counting solutions to polynomial equations, proved by Pierre Deligne in 1974 — in about 1.8 million lines of Lean, across 28,800 subagent invocations. But the post links only to Wikipedia pages: no repository, no paper, no statement file. There is no way to know which exact theorem is stated, under which axioms, or whether the code rebuilds. Especially since a public version already exists: Axiom published [AxiomMath/Weil](https://github.com/AxiomMath/Weil) on November 6, a week before Google's post, under Apache 2.0, with 213,159 lines and, according to its founder, less than a day of work. Google does not mention Axiom. The same caution applies to the other LEAP formalizations: Hilbert's tenth problem (919,000 lines), Birkhoff, 3D Bochner-Riesz — where the system reportedly found and fixed a gap in a recently released informal proof — and a reproduction of a 3D Navier-Stokes blowup proof. No public code linked.

The timeline needs correcting too: the Courtade-Kumar proof is not unique. Vu Khac Ky and Tuan Tran posted a different proof the same day ([arXiv:2609.24184](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.24184)), Mahdavifar and Beirami followed on September 22 — disclosing that their one-bit part "was developed by generative AI under our high-level direction" — then Kramer and Saglam of DeepInfra, and OpenAI with two more in October. Google's post mentions none of them.

### Why it matters

The engineering idea underneath the scorecard is more durable than the numbers: pair parallel attempts with objective falsifiers, partition contexts, and let a meta-system learn from failures — one long campaign concluded that tightly coupled code is better handled by sequential stages than by parallel splits, and that finding went into /boost. A harness tuning another harness, and that is transferable.

But the episode also shows the limit of such announcements: when verification is the product, the line count measures how hard the checker was worked, not how much mathematics was done. The 50 million lines are real — and they say something different from what the headline suggests.

### Who it is for

For researchers in formal mathematics and theoretical computer science, who can reuse the Courtade-Kumar repository and its certificates. For teams building multi-agent systems: the "agents + falsifiers" pattern is the takeaway. And for the 10,000 academic researchers Google is offering six months of free access to Antigravity's highest tier — /teamwork, /boost and Deep Review are now available externally.

### What we don't know yet

Whether Google's Weil formalization exists in a verifiable form, when its code will be published, and what it covers exactly. Whether the 1.8 million lines are a thin layer over unseen foundations or a self-contained development. And whether the other LEAP results — Zircon, libgav1, the Go engine — will ever come with their artifacts.

## 2. Smart glasses: privacy becomes a burden on bystanders

### What is it?

The EFF published on November 12 a piece with an explicit title — ["Tech Companies Are Trying to Shift the Entire Concept of Privacy (Again)"](https://www.eff.org/deeplinks/2026/11/tech-companies-are-trying-shift-entire-concept-privacy-again) — which reached 473 points on Hacker News. The finding: Meta's and Google's smart glasses can record anything the wearer is looking at or use AI to describe it. Apple's Audio Intelligence feature turns every Apple Watch into a transcription machine that records or summarizes your day. Amazon's Bee devices go a step further and can record and transcribe everything. Dozens of companies are adding pendants, rings and other products designed to record, transcribe or summarize everything we say and do, all day long.

### What actually changed

The EFF's central argument: privacy has always been a team sport — what you do with your messaging apps or your cloud backup already affects others. But moving those choices into the physical world, where strangers, colleagues, family and friends all become part of your privacy choices, drastically changes the game. Companies want us to accept a reality where one person's privacy choices (or lack of them) force everyone else to become an expert in consumer tech: understanding what each device can do, how it signals that it is recording, and which platforms it connects to.

The manufacturers' technical promises only cover the user. Apple points to a white paper on the technical barriers between the company and the watch's data. Meta talks about privacy controls and blurring faces when you interact with Meta AI through the glasses — but not during standard recording. Google does the same: bystanders' faces would be redacted before Gemini processes an image. Amazon says the user is "always in control" and no audio is stored — while offering a store integration so your conversations can directly turn into product ads. None of these promises protects the bystander: there is no technical protection for the person who finds themselves captured without using the device.

The legal framework, meanwhile, is starting to move. The European Data Protection Board adopted on October 13, 2026 a [statement on smart glasses](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/system/files/2026-11/20261013_edpb_012026_statement_smart-glasses_en.pdf): once they capture personal data, the GDPR applies, and their resemblance to ordinary eyewear makes collection "near invisible". BEUC, the European consumer organization, wrote on October 21 to Commissioner Virkkunen asking that covert surveillance through these glasses not become the norm in Europe, highlighting risks of harassment and non-consensual sharing — with women and children most at risk.

Google, for its part, opened preorders for its glasses on November 12, with availability announced for December 10 (Warby Parker, Gentle Monster, Samsung). Asked by CNN about an experimental system that would alert bystanders' phones when someone nearby is recording, a Google product director acknowledged the problem: "And just alerting bystanders doesn't do much to help. It can actually be weirdly disempowering to you in that you're like, 'What am I meant to do now?'"

### Why it matters

Because the question is no longer "should I accept being filmed?" but "who has to do the work?" Until now, recording someone took a visible gesture — pulling out a phone, raising a camcorder. The new wave is designed to encourage continuous monitoring and hide its use. The EFF puts it bluntly: if secretly documenting conversations becomes the norm, every private conversation becomes less spontaneous. Taking notes in a work meeting is completely normal. Getting a summary of a conversation with your best friend about their divorce is not.

### Who it is for

For everyone — and that is the problem: users and bystanders alike. For people who wear these devices out of accessibility needs — Meta highlights this use for blind and visually impaired people — and who will become the default targets of non-consensual recording. For employers, schools and care settings, which will have to set rules before the devices go mainstream.

### What we don't know yet

Whether bystander alerts will ever ship, and in what form. Whether European regulators will move from statements to a ban on remote biometric identification, as BEUC and the European Data Protection Supervisor demand. And whether the public, as the EFF suggests, will simply reject these products — the organization notes that entire product categories have vanished within a few years when nobody wanted them.

## 3. Project Zero: vulnerability disclosure moves to a fixed 90 days — then 60, then 30

### What is it?

Google Project Zero, the security research team whose disclosure policy is the industry's benchmark, announced on November 13 a complete rewrite of its rule: ["Policy and Disclosure: 2026 Edition"](https://projectzero.google/2026/11/disclosure-policy.html). Since 2021, the rule was "90 + 30": 90 days for the vendor to fix the flaw, then 30 extra days of "soak time" for the patch to roll out before technical details were published. The new rule is called "Full X": details are published X days after the bug is reported to the vendor, whether or not a patch exists.

### What actually changed

The schedule is phased: "Full 90" effective now, then "Full 60" on February 2, 2027, then "Full 30" on January 3, 2028. The soak period is gone, along with most special cases — the 14-day grace period and the variant policy. Severity no longer drives the disclosure decision: it is still tracked and communicated, but it is no longer a decision point in the timeline. The remaining exceptions are vulnerabilities actively exploited in the wild (handled under Google's application-security policy), already-public bugs, vendor requests to publish details, and rare hardware cases like Spectre and Meltdown.

The driver is AI on both sides. Project Zero explains that AI-assisted discovery and exploitation have driven patch-to-exploitation time to zero in some cases. The team now reports several vulnerabilities a week, versus a few a year previously. The 90+30 model, designed when disclosure was the exception, no longer holds when discovery is industrialized.

This is not the first acceleration: in July 2025, Project Zero launched "Reporting Transparency", publishing limited details within seven days — report date, deadline — with no information that would help an attacker.

### Why it matters

Because Project Zero's rule is the industry's metronome: when it changes, vendors, security teams and researchers align. A fixed 30-day deadline in 2028, with no soak time and no negotiation over severity, means patch teams will have to ship fast — or accept public disclosure of an unfixed flaw. It is also an admission: the race between discovery and exploitation is now won by whoever automates best, and the disclosure calendar has to follow the machines' pace, not committees'.

### Who it is for

For security teams and software vendors, who have a year to prepare for Full 60 and then Full 30. For security researchers, whose leverage is strengthened. For users, finally: faster disclosure means faster patching — or, failing that, faster information about the risk they face.

### What we don't know yet

How vendors will react when Full 30 arrives in 2028 — especially small vendors and open-source projects without a dedicated team. Whether other research teams will follow the same calendar. And whether removing the soak period will increase the number of flaws disclosed without a fix, or push vendors to fix faster.

## 4. The job market automates on both sides

### What is it?

Two stories from the same day, two sides of one coin. On the candidate side, [HiringNow](https://www.producthunt.com/products/hiringnow) is today's #1 Product Hunt launch: a job-search agent that monitors 247,000+ company career sites every five minutes, spots fresh openings — often before they reach the big job boards — tailors your resume and cover letter to each role, and applies directly on the employer's site, with your approval before anything is submitted. Ten free auto-applies, then $29 a month for 100, $49 for 300, $99 for 1,000. The site claims about 50,000 new jobs detected per day on average.

On the scam side, CBC revealed on November 12 that [more than 1,500 fake job listings](https://www.cbc.ca/news/canada/linkedin-monster-take-down-over-1-000-fake-job-postings-after-cbc-investigation-9.7415379) were removed from LinkedIn and Monster after its investigation. The ads promised remote travel jobs — up to $75,000 a year, no experience required — and funneled applicants to Evolution Travel, a US-based multi-level marketing company. There was no job and no salary: hundreds of dollars in upfront fees, then a monthly subscription, in exchange for the promise of earning money by booking travel and recruiting others. The listings used names like Wanderwell Collective, Professional Travel Hub and Sunkissed Getaways, never mentioning Evolution Travel or the fees.

### What actually changed

The CBC investigation builds on the work of Jay Jones, a Chicago job seeker who calls himself "The Profiler". Since losing his advertising job at the end of 2023, he has hunted fake listings on LinkedIn: he says he has helped get about 60,000 ads removed in two years, and flagged some 7,000 profiles. He spotted the problem in February 2026, finding fake travel agencies; by May, a single page carried 681 of them. His most worrying finding: scammers now use AI to tailor job pitches to a person's resume and career history. LinkedIn says it blocked nearly 90 million fake accounts before they went live in the second half of 2025, and has worked with the FBI since the summer to educate job seekers. A tool letting verified employers immediately remove a hijacked affiliation is expected later this month.

### Why it matters

Because recruitment automation does not only benefit honest people. On one side, an agent that applies on your behalf before everyone else; on the other, listings generated at scale, personalized by AI, exploiting exactly the same gaps — speed, scale, personalization. The job market is becoming a race between agents that apply and agents that trap. And verification — of an employer, a listing, a profile — becomes the skill that separates an opportunity from a scam.

### Who it is for

For job seekers first: a listing that asks you to pay to work is not a job. For platforms, which will have to prove they filter better than scammers generate. And for recruiters: when candidates arrive through agents, screening changes in nature — HiringNow says it itself, the agent only sends "what's true", and nothing goes out without human approval.

### What we don't know yet

How many fake listings remain online — Jay Jones says hundreds of active pages still lead to Evolution Travel or shell companies. Whether platforms will deliver on their promised instant-removal tools. And whether application agents, by multiplying submissions, will saturate recruiters to the point where a human-written resume becomes distinctive again.

## 5. PyPI: a Security tab to know where the packages your agents install come from

### What is it?

The [PyPI blog](https://blog.pypi.org/posts/2026-11-06-ui-updates-complete/) announced on November 6 a redesign of its project pages, with a new "Security" tab now live. The idea: make Python packages' security signals visible — in particular their provenance, the cryptographic proof of where each file in a release came from. Digital attestations, introduced in 2024, make it possible to verify a file's origin; the problem was that the interface barely showed them.

### What actually changed

Each release now gets a provenance state, computed by checking whether every file has an attestation. Two main states: "Full Provenance" when 100% of files are attested, "No Provenance" when none are — which does not mean the release is malicious, only that its origin cannot be cryptographically verified. Two attestation types are recognized: "PyPI Publish", confirming the file was published by a Trusted Publisher, and "SLSA Build Provenance", linking the file back to its exact source commit, build platform and instructions.

Four warnings appear when provenance looks unusual: "Incomplete" (some files attested, others not), "Inconsistent" (files from different build systems or source repositories), "Change of Provenance" (the release comes from a different repository or workflow than the previous one — often legitimate, like an organization rename, but sometimes a sign of a compromised pipeline) and "Loss of Provenance" (a project that used to publish attestations no longer does). Warnings sit at the top of the page, alongside yanked, archived and pre-release banners; a quarantine banner overrides everything. The release files and release history tabs were redesigned: search and filters, per-file provenance state, alerts on files uploaded more than 14 days after the initial release — a practice banned since July 2026.

The initiative is funded by the Open Source Security Foundation and implemented by Kabu Creative, following the OpenSSF Attestations Style Guide. And it is no coincidence that PyPI security is attracting resources: Brett Smith has joined the Python Software Foundation as Security Developer, a role funded by Anthropic.

### Why it matters

Because coding agents install packages without looking. When an agent runs `pip install` on a model's suggestion, it does not read metadata, check the maintainer's history, or notice that a release comes from a different repository than the previous one. Cryptographic provenance becomes the guardrail that replaces the human eye — provided it is visible. That is exactly what this tab does: turn a check nobody performed into a signal everyone sees.

### Who it is for

For Python developers, obviously — but above all for those who let agents install dependencies, which is to say, by now, almost everyone. For package maintainers: setting up Trusted Publishing and generating attestations is becoming the expected minimum. For security teams, who gain a standardized signal to build into their policies.

### What we don't know yet

What share of the most-installed packages will reach "Full Provenance", and how fast. Whether the warnings will be taken seriously or ignored like so many other alerts. And whether other registries — npm, crates.io, model hubs — will follow with equivalent tabs.

## Bonus — The random pick of the day: DeepSeek V3 fits in 190 lines of JAX

A developer posted on Hacker News today an implementation of the DeepSeek V3 architecture in about 190 lines of pure JAX — no Flax, no PyTorch — with its two signature ideas: Multi-head Latent Attention (MLA) and Mixture-of-Experts (MoE). He trained it from scratch on public-domain detective fiction, on a MacBook Air, at about 10,700 tokens per second, with validation loss dropping from 5.02 to 3.51.

Do not mistake the size: this miniature has 36.2 million parameters. The real DeepSeek V3 has 671 billion, with 37 billion activated per token. This is not a model to use, it is a model to read: a state-of-the-art architecture shrunk to a size a human can hold in their head, to understand how it works. The kind of object that makes AI legible — and a reminder of why minimal implementations remain one of the best ways to learn.

[HN thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/) — Show HN, November 14, 2026, 183 points at time of reading.

## What today tells us

- **Verification is becoming the product.** Google's Lean falsifiers, PyPI's attestations, Project Zero's disclosure calendar: in all three cases, the value is no longer in the announcement but in the proof that it can be checked — and in the fact that someone actually checked it.
- **AI is leaving software behind.** Glasses that film bystanders, agents that apply for jobs, packages installed by machines: the questions are no longer technical, they are social — consent, scams, trust.
- **The numbers you are sold need independent checking.** Fifty million lines of which 0.5% is readable proof, 1.8 million lines with no public code, 247,000 monitored sites: every number in this edition was counted, weighed or challenged by someone other than its author. That is a good habit to keep.
