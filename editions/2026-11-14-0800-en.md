## 1. ChatGPT Wallet: the agent can finally pay — without ever seeing your card

**What is it?** OpenAI announced ChatGPT Wallet on November 13, a wallet that lets its personal agents — the "dots", always-on agents introduced in September — pay for a purchase on your behalf. Built with American Express, Mastercard and Visa, OpenAI calls it "the first wallet that lets agents pay without access to users' actual card details and that requires a security check for every purchase."

**What actually changes:**
- **The agent never touches the card number.** The flow has three steps: you add a card once; you review the items, the total and the chosen card; you approve the purchase with a security check — Face ID or a fingerprint. Only after that approval does the payment network issue a token, a substitute card number with its own security code and expiry date. Neither the merchant nor the agent sees the real card details. Card rewards (points, miles) and protections — for a defective product, for example — are preserved.
- **The rollout is narrow, and that is a choice.** For now, only US ChatGPT Pro subscribers aged 18 and older, with an eligible US-issued card (American Express, Chase, Mastercard or Visa, with a US billing address), get access, and the rollout is gradual. Debit cards and virtual cards are not supported at launch. OpenAI has announced no date for international availability: it says only that it will "expand availability" after learning from early use.
- **OpenAI takes no cut — for now.** According to CNBC, as relayed by several analyses, OpenAI charges no commission on purchases at launch: the play is distribution, not fees. The company works with Forter, Riskified, Sardine and Signifyd on fraud detection, and with Adyen, Checkout.com and Stripe on the payment side. A Commerce Partner Playbook was published alongside for merchants.
- **The next step is already announced.** OpenAI says users will eventually be able to delegate purchases to agents within pre-authorised limits — spending caps by category or time window. For now, every purchase requires explicit approval: there is no delegation without a check.
- **The same day, the competition chose the opposite.** Gemini added Google Wallet as a read-only connected app inside Personal Intelligence: the assistant can read your spending, it cannot pay. Anthropic, meanwhile, launched a Personal Finance beta in Claude on November 5. Three labs, three readings of the same problem: OpenAI lets the agent pay, Google lets it look, Anthropic starts with money management.

**Why it matters:** until now, an agent could search, compare and fill a cart — then stop at the checkout, because paying meant handing a card number to a program. The wallet solves that last metre, and it solves it with tokenisation, an old payment technique, not with new trust placed in the agent. It is also the most concrete test yet of the agent economy: if agents buy, who is responsible when the purchase is wrong — the user who approved, the agent that chose, the merchant that sold? OpenAI's answer, for now, is procedural: a human approval for every purchase, a disposable token for every transaction. And the limit lies elsewhere: the wallet solves payment, not permission. Two days before the launch, United Airlines rewrote its contract of carriage to ban agent-made bookings — payment works, the door stays shut.

**Who it's for:** US Pro subscribers first — they are the ones who will test, for real, whether handing your card to an agent is worth the time saved. And merchants: the token shows up as an ordinary card, no specific integration is required, which means agents can buy anywhere a card works — except where they have been explicitly banned.

**What we don't know yet:** when the wallet leaves the United States, or when pre-authorised spending limits arrive — and therefore when the agent will truly pay alone, without approval every time. Nor what happens in a dispute when the agent picked the wrong item: card protection applies, but the sharing of responsibility between the user, the agent and OpenAI has not been tested. Nor whether other airlines, after United, will ban agent purchases in their terms.

## 2. Cursor: a key exposed at a vendor, a backdoored extension for 5,000 developers

**What is it?** Cursor, the AI code editor, published on November 13 the full account of its October security incident — and it is a supply-chain story, not a flaw in its own code. An attacker obtained an access key Cursor had issued to Fireworks AI, its inference and training vendor. According to Cursor, the key was "exposed by Fireworks". From there, the attacker copied data, found credentials, then planted malicious copies of Anthropic's Claude Code extension on Cursor's extension marketplace. Up to 5,000 users may have received the backdoored build.

**What actually changes:**
- **The entry point was a shared bucket.** The stolen key gave read access to a storage bucket Cursor used to share data with Fireworks. The bucket held historical coding-agent traces — mostly from 2025, from sessions not in Privacy Mode, which Cursor was permitted to use for training. Fewer than 0.05% of users had traces exposed. The problem: a small number of those traces contained credentials that had not been redacted — fewer than 0.01% of customers had API credentials exposed.
- **From read to write in three steps.** Using those credentials, the attacker reached some of Cursor's SaaS tools, then, on October 29, placed malicious, unauthorised copies of `Anthropic.claude-code` versions 2.1.312 and 2.1.313 (Windows and macOS) directly on the cache serving Cursor's VS Code extension marketplace. The backdoored builds carried an SSH backdoor: injected JavaScript that ran when the extension loaded, without any Claude conversation being needed. They were available for about 11 hours, from 02:27 to 13:35 Pacific Time on October 29. Cursor published a security notice on October 30, with indicators of compromise and remediation steps, and began notifying users and team administrators.
- **What was not touched matters as much as the rest.** Cursor says prompts and code from users in Privacy Mode, customer code repositories, production systems, the Cursor app itself and release signing keys were not affected. Anthropic did not publish these copies, and its product was not vulnerable; Open VSX never hosted the malware. The last attacker activity Cursor observed was on November 1, and it was blocked. The company says the incident is contained.
- **The break with the vendor is total.** Cursor moved all inference traffic off Fireworks, deactivated the key issued to the vendor, cut off Fireworks' access to every shared dataset, rebuilt its extension marketplace and expanded secret scanning and network restrictions.
- **Fireworks has its own, broader account.** In a November 9 update to its bulletin, Fireworks widened the scope of its October incident: after a first finding (unauthorised use of internal credentials, access to secrets stored for evaluation jobs), the company found impact to account and billing information and, for a limited set of accounts, to secrets, datasets and incomplete model weights. A third-party cloud provider hosting Fireworks R&D machines experienced a security incident on October 23; on October 24, the attacker reached Fireworks development machines. Card numbers, Fireworks says, are not stored and were not impacted; Inference, the Training SDK and Nexus — zero data retention by default — were not detected as impacted. Palo Alto Networks Unit 42 is doing the forensics, law enforcement has been notified, and the investigation continues. One attribution nuance remains: it is Cursor that says the key was "exposed by Fireworks" — Fireworks' bulletins do not name Cursor.

**Why it matters:** the incident fits in one sentence security teams have repeated for years: your security stops at your weakest vendor. A key issued to a partner, a shared bucket, unredacted agent traces — three ordinary decisions, none malicious, chaining together into a backdoored extension distributed through a trusted channel. The target was not a user, it was the distribution mechanism itself: when a developer installs an extension from their editor's marketplace, they check nothing — which is exactly why the channel is worth attacking. And the timeline is telling: the attack lasted weeks (data copied, credentials exploited, extension planted on October 29), but the malicious distribution lasted only 11 hours — because that is the visible part. The operational lesson is twofold: redact secrets in agent traces before sharing them, and treat every key issued to a vendor as a key that will one day leak.

**Who it's for:** Cursor users who installed or updated the Claude Code extension on October 29 — the October 30 notice gives the indicators and the verification steps. And every team that shares data with an AI vendor: agent traces, training sets, shared buckets. The question to ask this morning is not "are we Cursor?", it is "which keys have we issued, to whom, and what can they read?"

**What we don't know yet:** who the attacker is, or what they did with the data copied from the bucket — Cursor does not say. Nor whether other Fireworks customers were hit through the same key or other paths — Fireworks names no customer in its bulletins. Nor how many of the 5,000 potentially affected users actually installed the backdoored build, or whether the backdoor was exploited on any of them.

## 3. DeepSeek: the whole app becomes an API — and the client becomes open source

**What is it?** DeepSeek announced on November 13 that the capabilities behind its consumer app are now available through its API. Web search, URL reading, file analysis, voice recognition: everything the app did server-side becomes an API call parameter. At the same time, DeepSeek open-sourced, under the MIT licence, the chat client that uses these capabilities — a client that runs entirely in the browser, with no server.

**What actually changes:**
- **The app becomes a building block.** The tools are switched on with parameters in a call to the `deepseek-flash` model (which runs on DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash-1017): `deepseek_web_search` for cited web search — or multi-step "Deep Thinking Search" when reasoning mode is active — `deepseek_read_url` to read a URL pasted in the prompt, file analysis (PDF, Word, PowerPoint, images), automatic conversation titles, and input-output content safety checks. Voice and OCR are separate services: speech-to-text, text-to-speech and optical character recognition have their own endpoints.
- **Three protocols, one model.** The new capabilities work across three endpoints: Chat Completions (OpenAI-style), the Responses API, and a Messages endpoint compatible with Anthropic's format. The choice is strategic: a developer who already has Anthropic code can plug in DeepSeek without rewriting their calls.
- **The open-source client is a demo, not a product.** The `deepseek-ai/chat-demo` repository (TypeScript, MIT licence) rebuilds the app experience: multiple conversations, streaming replies, deep search, link reading, attachments, voice input, Markdown, KaTeX and Mermaid rendering, import of history exported from the DeepSeek app, light and dark themes, Chinese and English interfaces. The API key and conversations stay in the browser (localStorage and IndexedDB) — nothing goes through a third-party server. More than 1,140 stars in a day on GitHub. The page warns itself: this is a demonstration, not a production app, and importing a history replaces the existing one.
- **Two grey areas, written in the documentation.** Tool pricing is not published: the docs give no per-call price list for search, fetching or parsing — `deepseek-flash` tokens cost $0.15 per million input and $0.60 per million output off-peak, but tool costs add up with no visible price list. And public-facing services keep their compliance duties: content-safety review, model registration, AI labelling — DeepSeek provides the tool, not the compliance.

**Why it matters:** DeepSeek is walking the opposite path from its competitors. OpenAI and Anthropic built apps (ChatGPT, Claude) and then APIs; DeepSeek had a popular app and a cheap model, and it is now turning the whole app into a developer building block. For a small developer, that is a considerable shortcut: instead of assembling web search, PDF reading, OCR and voice from four vendors, you get the whole set in one call, on a model at $0.15 per million tokens. The price is dependence: the tools run on DeepSeek's servers, under its rules, and the real cost — tools included — is not published. The open-source client has a different function: it shows exactly what the API can do, ready to use, and it imports your history — an elegant way of telling users "your app can become your code".

**Who it's for:** developers building assistants with search and files who do not want to assemble five services — and who accept not knowing the tool prices before the bill. And the curious: the client runs in the browser, you can try it with your own key in a few minutes, with no server.

**What we don't know yet:** the price of the tools — search, fetching, parsing — which appears nowhere in the documentation. Nor the rate limits or regional availability. Nor whether the built-in tools will one day work with `deepseek-v4-pro`: for now, they are only announced on `deepseek-flash`.

## 4. Claude Code Projects: the coordinator turns proactive, the workers stay parallel

**What is it?** Anthropic updated Claude Code Projects, its system where one conversation drives several coding agents in parallel. The news, announced on November 13 by the @ClaudeDevs developer account, is simple to state: the coordinator no longer just waits to be asked — it answers directly in the project chat and posts status updates while the work threads progress. The developer no longer has to watch: the project comes to them.

**What actually changes:**
- **The conductor speaks first.** In a project, the user describes the work; Claude scopes it, delegates the pieces to parallel threads, reviews what comes back and assembles the result. Until now, you had to ask the coordinator where the work stood. Now it answers questions in the chat and posts status updates as threads progress. The November 13 thread, posted at 22:51 UTC, already had about 383,000 views by 03:00 UTC the next day.
- **Share one thread, not the whole project.** Each thread can now be shared via a read-only link with anyone signed in to Claude — except threads that accessed the local computer, which stay unshareable. A detail that says a lot: sharing stops exactly where machine access begins.
- **Commands to manage the flow.** A scheduled reminder can hide a thread until a set time; `/btw` opens a side thread for a tangent; `/fork` branches a new thread from the current one. The limit is set: 200 new threads per day across all projects.
- **Parallelism costs limits, not money.** Each thread is a full Claude Code cloud session, with its own copy of the repository and its own Git branch — and each thread draws on the same usage limits as other sessions. Several workers in parallel therefore burn through the allowance faster than a single session; a thread that hits a limit pauses and resumes automatically. New projects use Opus everywhere: high effort for worker threads, low effort for the coordinator. Cloud threads keep working after the laptop closes; when a task genuinely needs the local machine, the project can run that thread on the user's computer through Remote Control.
- **The scope stays narrow.** Projects run on claude.ai/code, the desktop app and the mobile app — not in the terminal, nor in the VS Code or JetBrains extensions. The gradual public beta covers Pro, Max and Ultra plans, with a waitlist; Team and Enterprise plans are not covered yet. An important reminder: the Projects redesign — one conversation, a coordinator, parallel threads — dates from September 17. The November 13 news is the proactive coordinator and the controls, not the parallelism itself.

**Why it matters:** the "coordinator + workers" shape is becoming the default way to run many agents inside the most-used coding harness — two weeks after Nous Research and GitHub published receipts for agents delegating to other agents. The change is not technical, it is ergonomic: the cost of a multi-agent system is no longer launching the agents, it is watching them. A coordinator that reports on its own removes the most thankless task — going to check whether the workers have finished — and moves human work to what remains: deciding, reviewing, arbitrating. But the limit sits in the business model: every worker consumes the same allowance as the user, and parallelism turns a weekly limit into an afternoon's limit. The question is no longer "how many agents can I launch?", it is "how many can I afford to run at once?"

**Who it's for:** developers who already hand whole features to Claude Code — they gain a conductor that reports on its own. And teams still hesitating: read-only sharing of a thread lets you show an agent's work without handing over the keys to the project.

**What we don't know yet:** when Team and Enterprise plans will get Projects, or when threads will be able to run locally with tools and code behind the user's network — Anthropic says "very soon". Nor what the proactive coordinator is worth on long projects, where updates can turn into noise.

## 5. Feature — Open weights fight back: a 65B model on a desktop machine, a local engine at 1.0, a 27B model on 12 GB of memory

**What is it?** Three announcements in one week tell the same story: open models are no longer curiosities for researchers, they are becoming infrastructure you can install, version and run at home. A dense 65-billion-parameter model that fits on a desktop machine; the most-used local inference engine finally reaching version 1.0; a 27-billion-parameter model compressed until it runs on 12 GB of memory. And in the background, a small Thinking Machines model taking the top of the open leaderboards.

**What actually changes:**
- **Ripple-65B Preview: the dense model that beats giants — by its own numbers.** On November 4, Ricursive Intelligence, a lab building AI for chip design, released Ripple-65B Preview: 65 billion parameters, all active for every token (dense, not mixture-of-experts), trained from scratch on 12 trillion tokens, Apache 2.0 licence, one-million-token context. The headline score: 80.52 on Terminal-Bench 2.1, presented as the best score among US open-weight models — ahead of Reflection Beam 501B (80.1), Muse Spark Open 816B (76.4), Inkling 975B (63.8) and Nemotron 3 Ultra 550B (56.4). The claimed R&D cost: $32 million, for a five-person team. BF16 weights weigh about 130 GB; an experimental NVFP4 build of 57 GB fits on an NVIDIA DGX Spark, Nvidia's desktop AI machine. On performance, Ricursive claims 250 tokens per second on two B200s, at batch size 8. The model is hosted at $0.20 per million input tokens and $0.60 per million output (Together AI, OpenRouter). The caveats sit in Ricursive's own table: the model trails on SWE-bench Verified, DeepSWE, SciCode and MCP Atlas, and no independent evaluation had been published by November 5. Ricursive is led by Anna Goldie (CEO) and Azalia Mirhoseini (CTO), known for AlphaChip, Google's system that designs TPU layouts with reinforcement learning.
- **llama.cpp 1.0.0: the local engine becomes a contract.** On November 13, llama.cpp and its ggml tensor library moved to version 1.0.0 — three and a half years after the project was created, on March 10, 2023. The tag was cut at 10:20 UTC, the Hugging Face blog post went out at 14:05 UTC. The news is not a performance gain, it is a promise: from now on, any breaking change to the public C API will only arrive with a major-version bump. Version 1.0 cleans house: 35 deprecated functions removed, along with the warmup setter and the defragmentation threshold; the NextN speculative-decoding API promoted into the main header; FP8 key-value caches — a 32,768-token cache for Qwen2.5-7B falls from 1,792 to 896 MiB; a host-memory cache for MoE experts; llama-server hardened against SSRF and DNS rebinding. The project supports more than 150 model architecture variants, has 134,858 stars and 25,565 forks, under the MIT licence. An official release tag is only created when the internal copy of ggml matches the released ggml exactly — which keeps Debian and Homebrew compatible. Hugging Face also announced Llama desktop apps (llama.app) built on llama.cpp, to cut setup down to a few clicks. The criticism that keeps coming back in the comments: the project has "a ton of open issues and PRs" and lacks maintainers to keep up the pace.
- **Unsloth Qwen3.8-27B, Dynamic V3.1: the 27B model that fits in a pocket.** On November 11, Unsloth released new GGUF quantisations of Qwen3.8-27B — a dense 27-billion-parameter model with vision and reasoning, 256K context — using its Dynamic V3.1 method. The claimed accuracy gain: 5 to 10% at the same size on Terminal Bench, DeepSWE and LiveCodeBench v6. The smallest file, at 1-bit, weighs 6.19 GB and runs on setups with 12 GB of RAM (17 GB of RAM/VRAM per the documentation); the 4-bit build runs on 16 to 19 GB of VRAM — an RTX 5080, a 4090, a Mac with 24 GB of unified memory. Base model scores: 73.0 on Terminal Bench 2.1, 90.3 on LiveCodeBench v6, 61.7 on SWE-bench Pro. The 3-bit quantisations were not updated — they performed on par. The method profiles how sensitive each layer is to quantisation error: sensitive weights stay at high precision, the rest are compressed harder. All these numbers are Unsloth's, not independently verified.
- **In the background: Inkling 2 Small.** Thinking Machines' small model's open weights arrived on the Hub this week; it ranks #1 among US open models on Code Arena WebDev (1,647). It uses a native NVFP4 forward pass from pretraining onward — 3.6× fewer bits than BF16, up to 6× faster on GB300.

**Why it matters:** each announcement attacks a different lock on the same wall. Ripple says a small, well-trained dense model can rival giants eight to fifteen times its size — and that $32 million is enough to train it from scratch. llama.cpp says local infrastructure is now mature enough to promise stability: a version number that means something is what lets applications build on top of it without fearing the next update. Unsloth says compression is no longer a shameful compromise but a discipline: 5 to 10% accuracy gained at the same size means a 27-billion model becomes usable on an ordinary laptop. Together, the three moves trace the same trajectory: open AI is leaving the lab to become software you install — with its versions, its compatibility contracts and its measurable trade-offs. The other side is written in the sources themselves: Ripple's and Unsloth's scores are self-reported, and llama.cpp is short of maintainers at the very moment everyone depends on it.

**Who it's for:** developers who want to run a model at home — Ripple on a DGX Spark, Qwen3.8-27B on a 12 GB laptop — and teams building local applications on llama.cpp: version 1.0 finally gives them a stability contract, provided they check what was removed (35 deprecated functions will no longer compile).

**What we don't know yet:** whether Ripple's score will hold up against independent evaluations — the cited benchmark, Terminal-Bench 2.1, is not the newest version, and the comparison was run by Ricursive itself. Nor whether Unsloth will keep its 5 to 10% lead outside its own test benches. Nor whether llama.cpp will find the maintainers it needs to carry a 1.0 that the whole local ecosystem uses.

## Bonus — The luck of the day: the "No AI Slop" manifesto, or when developers set rules against their own agents

**What is it?** Speakers and attendees of the AIE Code Summit 2026 published a manifesto — "No AI Slop" — which reached the Hacker News front page on November 13 (189 points the next day). The text explicitly presents itself as the heir to the 2001 Agile Manifesto: seventeen engineers gathered in a ski lodge to answer the heavyweight processes of their time; twenty-five years later, developers gathering to answer mass production by agents.

**What it says:** the manifesto defines "slop" in one sentence: "Slop is when we put less effort into producing something than we ask the consumer to spend understanding it." It sets out five values: outcomes over output; understanding over speed; our own thinking over a model's first draft; better work over easier work; improving the system over patching the output. And eighteen beliefs, three of which summarise the text: "a swarm of agents can produce more work in a single day than a person can review in our lifetime"; "the cost of producing a thousand-line PR or a ten-page doc is approaching zero, while the cost of reviewing it remains constant"; "we own the intent, taste, and consequences of what we ship." The text also warns that slop compounds: low-quality content accumulates in knowledge bases and repositories, and models trained on it perpetuate it. The most quoted line in the comments: "AI cannot replace human judgement, it can only amplify our judgement (or lack thereof)."

**The lesson that goes with it:** the manifesto arrives at the exact moment this edition tells of agents that pay, code in parallel and produce faster than anyone can review. It is not an anti-AI text — its first belief says AI is "the most useful tool most of us have ever worked with". It is a text about the bottleneck that remains: human attention. When producing costs zero and reviewing still costs the same, the only variable we control is what we choose to produce — and what we choose to review. The signatories propose a simple rule, almost banal, and hard to keep: when someone sends you slop, tell them, kindly, and ask them to review it first.

**Who it's for:** every team that lets agents write code, documents or messages — and that is starting to feel the review queue grow faster than the production queue.

## What this day tells us

- **Agents reach the checkout — and the gates.** ChatGPT Wallet solves payment through tokenisation, with a human approval for every purchase. But United had already shut the door to agents two days earlier: money follows agents, rules precede them. The agent economy will be fought as much in contracts of carriage as in wallets.
- **Security has become the price of admission for agents.** A key exposed at a vendor, a backdoored extension distributed through a trusted channel, weights that cannot be proven clean: the Cursor incident is a reminder that every link — vendor, traces, marketplace — is an attack surface. Yesterday IBM proposed separating instructions from data inside the format itself; today the lesson is more down to earth: redact secrets before sharing, and treat every issued key as a key that will leak.
- **Open weights are becoming infrastructure, with contracts and bills.** Ripple, llama.cpp 1.0 and Unsloth say the same thing from three angles: a model you can install, a version that promises stability, compression that fits on a laptop. Meanwhile, Anthropic's IPO is reportedly about six times oversubscribed according to Bloomberg — money, for its part, keeps following closed models. Both worlds are advancing at once, and that is not a contradiction: it is the same market, seen from both sides.
